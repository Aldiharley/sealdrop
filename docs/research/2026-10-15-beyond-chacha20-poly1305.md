# Keep ChaCha20-Poly1305, upgrade the keys around it

**No cipher change is warranted: nothing meaningfully stronger than ChaCha20-Poly1305 exists for sealdrop to adopt, and swapping it would cost interoperability while leaving every real limit untouched.** The best published key-recovery attack reaches **7.5 of ChaCha20's 20 rounds at 2^242 work**, no standards body rates the cipher cryptographically below AES-GCM (the gaps are FIPS/CNSA approval status, not strength), and the 2026 editions of the practitioner "right answers" lists from Latacora and from age's own author still recommend the ChaCha family for application-layer encryption. Every property an alternative AEAD could add, from nonce-misuse resistance to larger nonces to higher per-key data limits, is already neutralised by age's design, which derives a fresh 256-bit payload key per file. The actual ceilings on a sealdrop package sit elsewhere: the **128-bit age file key** (the whole system is 128-bit secure whichever 256-bit AEAD encrypts the bytes), the **X25519 recipient stanza** that a future quantum computer can open retroactively, the Ed25519 manifest signature, passphrase entropy in password mode, and custody of the minisign key and release service. For packages that must stay confidential as well as openable for 10+ years, the single change with a hard deadline is post-quantum key wrapping: sealdrop must encrypt to and decrypt from age's native `age1pq` recipients, with no X25519 fallback stanza, before the 2030 line that NIST, the EU, the UK, Australia and the US Executive Order converge on, and that capability is currently blocked on the Rust `age` crate. The recommended path, in priority order, is (1) a PQ-only recipient mode including decryption, (2) a manifest-level key-commitment field that costs no interop, (3) hardening of signing-key custody, the release service and parsers, (4) a hybrid Ed25519 + ML-DSA-65 manifest signature by 2031, and (5) a sealdrop payload format v2 built on Cobblestone-128 under a sealdrop version line, but only if a concrete FIPS/AES or committing-payload requirement ever materialises. Cipher cascades and "paranoid modes" should be declined outright.

## Twelve unbroken rounds and no standards-body demerit

The owner's request assumes a stronger cipher exists to move to. On the cryptanalytic record it does not. The Eurocrypt 2025 bit-puncturing work gave "the first 7.5-round attack with a non-negligible advantage over an exhaustive search" at roughly 2^250 time ([ePrint 2025/437](https://eprint.iacr.org/2025/437)), and a September 2026 refinement pushed 7.5-round ChaCha256 to **2^242.02** and 7 rounds to 2^142.08 ([ePrint 2026/1837](https://eprint.iacr.org/2026/1837)). That leaves 12.5 of 20 rounds untouched, the attacks are incremental refinements of one differential-linear technique with no sign of reaching 8 rounds, and even the reduced-round cost sits far above the 2^128 that the rest of the system offers. AES-256 is not in better shape by the same yardstick: the only full-round single-key result is still the 2011 biclique at **2^254.4**, a 1.6-bit saving over brute force ([ePrint 2011/449](https://eprint.iacr.org/2011/449)), non-biclique single-key attacks stop at 9 of 14 rounds, and the AES-256 key schedule is the structural weak point that every practical related-key attack exploits, a model that never arises in age because every key is fresh random or HKDF output. RFC 8439 states the design target plainly: "The ChaCha20 cipher is designed to provide 256-bit security" ([RFC 8439](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8439.html)).

Regulators draw the same line between approval and strength. BSI TR-02102-1 (January 2026) does not list ChaCha20 at all, but its landing page says unlisted mechanisms "are not necessarily considered by the BSI to be insecure", and its own IPsec part accepts ChaCha20-Poly1305 ([BSI TR-02102-1](https://www.bsi.bund.de/SharedDocs/Downloads/EN/BSI/Publications/TechGuidelines/TG02102/BSI-TR-02102-1.pdf?__blob=publicationFile&v=10)). ANSSI's March 2026 rules declare ChaCha20 conformant to both its classical and its post-quantum stream-cipher rules ([ANSSI v3.00](https://messervices.cyber.gouv.fr/documents-guides/anssi-guide-mecanismes-crypto-3.00.pdf)). The IRTF treats the two AEADs as peers and, in the one place it differentiates them, favours ChaCha20-Poly1305: the single-key confidentiality bound is "no limit beyond the PRF security of the underlying ChaCha20 block function", whereas AES-GCM carries a birthday term ([draft-irtf-cfrg-aead-limits](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/draft-irtf-cfrg-aead-limits)). The only bodies that rank AES above are FIPS 140 and NSA CNSA 2.0, and age's author anticipated that buyer years ago: "It is somewhat unlikely that an entity with opinions about AES vs ChaCha20 will be ok with X25519, too, so a 'checks the boxes' version would need more changes" ([age discussion #368](https://github.com/FiloSottile/age/discussions/368)). A compliance-driven AES swap would not actually deliver compliance.

Practitioner consensus has drifted toward the ChaCha family, not away from it. Latacora's post-quantum edition, updated 24 June 2026, recommends NaCl secretbox and then "XChaCha20Poly1305" when AAD is needed, with 256-bit keys, and says worrying about AES-GCM key wear-out "is time better spent elsewhere" ([Latacora](https://www.latacora.com/blog/post-quantum-cryptographic-right-answers/)). Filippo Valsorda's 12 October 2026 list names "XAES-256-GCM or XChaCha20Poly1305 with random nonces" as the symmetric defaults, recommends age itself for multi-recipient file encryption, and notes committing AEADs "are not commonly available yet" ([words.filippo.io](https://words.filippo.io/right-answers/)). The table below summarises what each realistic alternative would add and whether age's construction already makes the property moot.

| Candidate | Property it adds over ChaCha20-Poly1305 | Load-bearing in age? | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| AES-256-GCM | Hardware speed; FIPS/CNSA approval | No: adds a birthday term and a 64 GiB-per-nonce cap; still 128-bit file key; needs X25519 replaced too for compliance | [aead-limits](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/draft-irtf-cfrg-aead-limits), [#368](https://github.com/FiloSottile/age/discussions/368) |
| AES-256-GCM-SIV | Nonce-misuse resistance | No: payload key is unique per file, chunk nonces are a counter, wrap keys are single-use | [RFC 8452](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8452.html), [age spec](https://github.com/C2SP/C2SP/blob/main/age.md) |
| XChaCha20-Poly1305 | 192-bit nonce safe for random use | No: age never generates random AEAD nonces | [Latacora](https://www.latacora.com/blog/post-quantum-cryptographic-right-answers/) |
| Ascon-AEAD128 | Key commitment | Partly, but 128-bit key and nonce make it weaker on the multi-key axis; standardised for constrained devices | [SP 800-232](https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/sp/800/232/final), [RFC 9771](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9771.html) |
| AEGIS-128L/256 | Speed with AES-NI; commitment in a restricted model | Marginal: nonce reuse leaks internal state, slow and side-channel-exposed without hardware AES, informational-track only | [AEGIS draft](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/draft-irtf-cfrg-aegis-aead) |
| Cascade (e.g. Serpent over ChaCha) | Hedge against total break | No: cascades can be weaker under CCA, Latacora lists them under "Avoid", and no source rates a ChaCha break as a live risk | [Green](https://blog.cryptographyengineering.com/2012/02/02/multiple-encryption/), [Latacora 2018](https://www.latacora.com/blog/2018/04/03/cryptographic-right-answers/) |

The reason the right-hand column is so uniform is structural. age derives the payload key by HKDF-SHA-256 from a fresh 128-bit file key and a fresh 16-byte nonce, encrypts 64 KiB chunks under an 11-byte counter nonce with a final-chunk marker, and wraps the file key in each stanza under a single-use key with a fixed zero nonce, which is the textbook case where a constant nonce is safe ([C2SP age spec](https://github.com/C2SP/C2SP/blob/main/age.md)). The STREAM construction it uses carries a proof that truncation and reordering of segments are rejected, with loss only q/2^τ for q segments and τ-bit tags ([ePrint 2015/189](https://eprint.iacr.org/2015/189)). Per-key data limits, random-nonce collision risk and multi-user degradation of the AEAD are all defeated before the cipher is even invoked. Performance does not change the calculus either: tuned AES-256-GCM is roughly 1.5x to 3x faster per core on recent x86 and Apple silicon ([Vardanian 2025](https://ashvardanian.com/posts/chacha-vs-aes-2025/)), but untuned AES-GCM on an M1 was 3x *slower* than ChaCha-Poly ([wolfSSL](https://www.wolfssl.com/cryptographic-benchmarks-new-apple-m1/)), and at either rate a 100 MB package encrypts in well under a second, below the noise of tar, SHA-256 hashing for the manifest, disk I/O and, in passphrase mode, scrypt or Argon2id.

## A 128-bit file key and X25519 set the real ceiling

If "stronger encryption" means a higher security level, the lever is not the AEAD. age's file key "MUST be generated as 16 bytes of CSPRNG output" and every other key, including the 256-bit payload key, is an HKDF derivation of it ([C2SP age spec](https://github.com/C2SP/C2SP/blob/main/age.md)). A sealdrop package is therefore **128-bit secure against key search** no matter which 256-bit cipher wraps the bytes; replacing ChaCha20-Poly1305 with AES-256-GCM would not move that number. The 256-bit payload key still earns its keep, because it is what makes the multi-target bound on the AEAD irrelevant, and the per-file nonce salted into HKDF is what stops an attacker with 2^64 files of known prefix from finding one decryption in 2^64 work, the rationale Valsorda gave when a longer file key was proposed ([Stäuble 2022](https://ethz.ch/content/dam/ethz/special-interest/infk/inst-infsec/appliedcrypto/education/theses/semester-project_mirco-stauble.pdf)). The same 128-bit level is exactly what TLS 1.3's mandatory suite, Signal's X3DH and WireGuard target, and NIST SP 800-57 rates 128-bit security acceptable "2019 – 2030 & beyond" ([keylength.com](https://www.keylength.com/en/4/)). Any sealdrop wording that says "256-bit encryption" should read "128-bit security level, 256-bit keys".

Quantum computing does not change the symmetric picture. Grover gives at most a quadratic speed-up, and under NIST's MAXDEPTH limit on serial circuit depth the most optimised AES-256 key-search circuit still costs about 1.39·2^221 gates at MAXDEPTH 2^64 ([Jaques et al.](https://eprint.iacr.org/2019/1146)); the one ChaCha-specific estimate lands at 1.233·2^251 gates at MAXDEPTH 2^40 ([Bathe, Anand, Dutta](https://digitalcommons.isical.ac.in/journal-articles/1669)). NIST's FAQ says Grover "will provide little or no advantage in attacking AES" and that even "AES 128 will remain secure for decades to come" ([NIST PQC FAQ](https://csrc.nist.gov/projects/post-quantum-cryptography/faqs)); NIST IR 8547 states that all approved symmetric primitives with at least 128 bits of classical security meet at least Category 1 ([NIST IR 8547](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/ir/2024/NIST.IR.8547.ipd.pdf)); and Valsorda argued in April 2026 that no symmetric key sizes need to change, warning that "Conflating necessary and unnecessary changes will cause needless churn" ([words.filippo.io/128-bits](https://words.filippo.io/128-bits/)). No standards body recommends keys longer than 256 bits.

The quantum threat is real, but it lives in the public-key layer, and it is asymmetric in time. Each X25519 stanza wraps the file key under a Diffie-Hellman secret that Shor's algorithm recovers; an adversary who stores a sealdrop package today can open it whenever a cryptographically relevant quantum computer exists, which is the "harvest now, decrypt later" scenario the CISA/NSA/NIST factsheet warns about. The March 2026 Google Quantum AI estimate cut the cost of breaking 256-bit elliptic-curve discrete logs to under 500,000 physical qubits and roughly 20 minutes of runtime, about 20x below the 2023 figure ([QRL summary](https://www.theqrl.org/blog/two-papers-just-changed-the-quantum-threat-conversation-heres-what-they-actually-say/)), and Cloudflare moved its own full-PQ target to 2029 in response ([Cloudflare roadmap](https://blog.cloudflare.com/post-quantum-roadmap/)). The Ed25519 manifest signature is the other classical primitive, but forgeries are only possible prospectively, after such a machine exists, and verifiers can be re-keyed, so its deadline is genuinely later. For a package produced in 2027 that must remain confidential to 2040, the exposure window on an X25519-only stanza is 13 years, and the mandates reflect that split: NIST IR 8547 deprecates 112-bit RSA/ECC after 2030 and disallows EdDSA and all ECDH-class key establishment after 2035 ([NIST IR 8547](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/ir/2024/NIST.IR.8547.ipd.pdf)); Executive Order 14412 (22 June 2026) requires PQC key establishment on federal high-value systems by **31 December 2030** and PQC signatures by 31 December 2031 ([EO 14412](https://www.whitehouse.gov/presidential-actions/2026/06/securing-the-nation-against-advanced-cryptographic-attacks/)); the EU roadmap says quantum-vulnerable public-key mechanisms "shall not be used stand-alone after the end of 2030" ([EU roadmap](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/library/coordinated-implementation-roadmap-transition-post-quantum-cryptography)); Australia's ISM says cease RSA, DH, ECDH and ECDSA by end of 2030 and commence transition by end of 2028 ([ASD](https://www.cyber.gov.au/business-government/secure-design/planning-post-quantum-cryptography)); the UK NCSC schedules high-priority upgrades 2028 to 2031 and completion by 2035 ([NCSC](https://www.ncsc.gov.uk/news/pqc-migration-roadmap-unveiled)). None binds a private tool directly, but OMB M-26-15 directs agencies to prioritise PQC-capable vendors ([OMB M-26-15](https://www.whitehouse.gov/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/M-26-15-Execution-of-the-Migration-to-Post-Quantum-Cryptography.pdf)), so these dates flow down through procurement.

age has already solved this at the stanza layer, which is the correct layer. Version 1.3.0 (27 December 2025) added a native `mlkem768x25519` recipient (`age1pq1…` keys) that seals the file key with HPKE using the X-Wing-identical ML-KEM-768 + X25519 hybrid KEM, HKDF-SHA256 and ChaCha20-Poly1305 ([age v1.3.0](https://github.com/FiloSottile/age/releases/tag/v1.3.0)), whose shared secret binds both KEM outputs plus the X25519 transcript through SHA3-256 so that it is secure "if SHA3 is secure, and either X25519 is secure, or ML-KEM-768 is secure" ([X-Wing draft](https://www.ietf.org/archive/id/draft-connolly-cfrg-xwing-kem-07.txt)). The spec adds the one rule that matters for sealdrop's PQ mode: the same file "SHOULD NOT be encrypted to both this recipient type and to other non-quantum-resistant recipient types", because a classical fallback stanza hands the harvester the file key anyway ([C2SP age spec](https://github.com/C2SP/C2SP/blob/main/age.md)). Here is the gap in sealdrop today. The Rust `age` crate 0.12.1 that sealdrop pins exposes only `tagpq::Recipient`, encryption-only, for the tagged `age1tagpq1…` hardware variant (`mlkem768p256tag`, which sealdrop's manifest calls `hybrid-pq`); it has no `age1pq` recipient and no PQ identity of any kind ([age CHANGELOG](https://github.com/str4d/rage/blob/main/age/CHANGELOG.md)). The maintainer has said the `pq` recipient and identity will arrive in rage 0.13.0, with no date ([rage #621](https://github.com/str4d/rage/issues/621)). Consequently a sealdrop user can produce a PQ package that only Go age or typage can open, which is also why 19 PQ vectors are skipped in sealdrop's CCTV run. Go age and typage 0.3.x both implement encrypt and decrypt natively ([typage releases](https://github.com/FiloSottile/typage/releases)), and age ships `age-plugin-pq` so that "any version and implementation of age that supports plugins" can use hybrid identities ([age README](https://github.com/FiloSottile/age)), which sealdrop can reach today because its `age` dependency already enables the `plugin` feature.

## Auditors flag signing keys, escrow and parsers before any primitive

The four closest audit precedents never produced a cipher finding. Radically Open Security's 2024 Picocrypt review found one Moderate issue, an ignored `rand.Read` error that would yield "a trivial key (all 0's)", plus an unauthenticated header, an unchecked negative length, and decrypt-before-MAC ([ROS report](https://github.com/Picocrypt/storage/blob/main/Picocrypt.Audit.Report.pdf)). Cure53's 2017 Cryptomator audit found one Critical: the release-signing private key committed to public repositories ([Cure53](https://cryptomator.org/audits/2017-11-27%20crypto%20cure53.pdf)). The only advisory across age, rage and typage in six years is a plugin-name path traversal that let attacker-controlled recipient strings execute arbitrary binaries ([GHSA-32gq-x56h-299c](https://github.com/FiloSottile/age/security/advisories/GHSA-32gq-x56h-299c), [RUSTSEC-2024-0433](https://rustsec.org/advisories/RUSTSEC-2024-0433)). Parsers, RNG handling, signing-key custody and OS interaction are where tools like sealdrop actually break.

Mapped onto sealdrop, that ordering puts the **minisign signing key** first: age provides no sender authentication by design ("age is in the business of integrity and confidentiality, not authentication"), so the signed manifest is the entire authenticity story, and a leaked or unpinned key collapses it ([words.filippo.io](https://words.filippo.io/dispatches/age-authentication/)). The **release service** comes second. A service holding a wrapping key is a key-escrow agent, and the comparison set shows how much weaker that is than the alternatives: Bitwarden Send keeps the key in the URL fragment, which "is not sent to the server" ([Bitwarden](https://bitwarden.com/help/send-encryption/)), and tlock/drand distributes the time-lock across a threshold network that still admits early decryption by a colluding threshold ([drand docs](https://docs.drand.love/docs/timelock-encryption/)). sealdrop's design is better than the generic escrow case, and this deserves to be stated precisely: the `sealdrop-release` stanza encrypts the *real recipients' stanzas* under `K_release`, so the service key is necessary but not sufficient, and a compromised service alone yields nothing without the recipient's X25519 identity or passphrase ([sealdrop format v1 §4.2](https://github.com/Aldiharley/sealdrop/blob/main/spec/sealdrop-format-v1.md)). What a compromise *does* defeat is the timing window itself, retroactively and silently, for every package whose ciphertext the attacker also holds, and the service necessarily learns release IDs, requester addresses and first-unlock times. The decoder's existing check that the stanza's `release_id` and service match the signed manifest is the right control against re-pointing a package at a rogue service; it is worth more than any cipher change.

Passphrase mode is bounded by entropy, not by the KDF. A single RTX 4090 ran hashcat's scrypt benchmark at 7,126 guesses per second at N = 2^14 ([Chick3nman gist](https://gist.github.com/Chick3nman/32e662a5bb63bc4f51b847bb422222fd)); scaling linearly to age's N = 2^18 gives roughly 445 per second, and even applying hashcat 7.0's claimed "+320%" scrypt speed-up ([hashcat v7.0.0](https://github.com/hashcat/hashcat/releases/tag/v7.0.0)) yields an upper bound near 2,000 per second, an estimate rather than a measurement because the 256 MiB per guess at age's parameters collapses GPU occupancy far more than the benchmark vector does. At 2,000 guesses per second a 28-bit human password falls in about a day and a half, 40 bits takes 17 years, and five Diceware words (64.6 bits) take 4·10^8 years. Raising scrypt from log N 18 to 20 buys about two bits; one extra Diceware word buys 12.9. Argon2id, which sealdrop ships as an optional stanza at the RFC 9106 recommended parameters ([RFC 9106](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9106.html)), is a defensible modern default for its side-channel properties but is not a security lever on its own; a passphrase generator in the UI and NIST's 15-character minimum ([SP 800-63B-4](https://pages.nist.gov/800-63-4/sp800-63b.html)) are.

The one cryptographic criticism of age that survives scrutiny is key commitment, and sealdrop has already mostly closed it. age's stanza wrap is not committing; age's own source accepts this as a one-bit loss relevant only to an online decryption oracle ([age scrypt.go](https://github.com/FiloSottile/age/blob/main/scrypt.go)), and the spec's exactly-32-byte body rule exists "to mitigate partitioning oracle attacks" ([C2SP age spec](https://github.com/C2SP/C2SP/blob/main/age.md)). Stäuble's 2022 ETH project could not break age's robustness but did craft two files differing in one bit of the unauthenticated payload nonce that decrypt to different valid plaintexts, a trick that requires the attacker to already know the file key ([Stäuble 2022](https://ethz.ch/content/dam/ethz/special-interest/infk/inst-infsec/appliedcrypto/education/theses/semester-project_mirco-stauble.pdf)). sealdrop's manifest signs the SHA-256 of the package file's exact bytes, nonce included, so two such files cannot both match one signature; the scenario is closed at the tool level for anyone who verifies before decrypting. The residual gap is that the signed hash commits the ciphertext to a signer, not to a key. The cheap fix, modelled on Cobblestone's HKDF-derived commitment that decoders "MUST check ... before continuing" ([C2SP chunked-encryption](https://github.com/C2SP/C2SP/blob/main/chunked-encryption.md)), is a manifest field `HKDF-SHA-256(file key, info = "sealdrop/commit/v1")` that sealdrop checks after unwrapping and before releasing plaintext; stock age ignores it, interop is untouched, and because the value derives from the random 128-bit file key rather than from the passphrase it does not create the second attack path that the Picocrypt auditors flagged in PCC-006.

## The upgrade path: post-quantum recipients first, payload format last

The ordering below follows the evidence: the only item with a confidentiality deadline is key wrapping, the only item with zero interop cost is commitment, and a payload change is a new format whether or not it changes the cipher. Every transition that has succeeded in the cited set (age 1.1 to 1.3, OpenSSH's four-year hybrid-KEX rollout, Signal's PQXDH then SPQR, Cryptomator's vault format 8) put a whole-version identifier at the top, kept reading old data forever, and shipped new KEMs as hybrids before touching the payload; the cautionary case is OpenPGP, where bundling key-format and payload-format changes produced the RFC 9580 versus LibrePGP split ([RFC 9580](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9580.html), [OpenSSH release notes](https://www.openssh.org/releasenotes.html), [Cryptomator](https://cryptomator.org/blog/2021/10/11/vault-format-8/)).

| Priority | Change | What it fixes | Interop cost | Timing |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **PQ-only recipient mode**: encrypt to and decrypt from `age1pq1…` (`mlkem768x25519`); refuse to mix with X25519 stanzas; add manifest kind for the native type (a compatible change under spec §10) | Harvest-now-decrypt-later on every long-lived package | None: standard age stanza, openable by age 1.3+ | Ship when rage 0.13 lands; until then bridge via `age-plugin-pq` through the already-enabled `plugin` feature; default-on by end 2028, X25519-only refused for new packages by end 2030 |
| 2 | **Manifest key-commitment field** derived from the file key via HKDF, checked before plaintext release | Residual non-commitment of stanza wraps; multi-key stanza games; implementation bugs | None: stock age ignores the field | Next minor release |
| 3 | **Operational hardening**: minisign key custody and pinning UX; release-service retention and logging; archive path normalisation; fail-closed on every CSPRNG error; decrypt-side KDF caps on untrusted packages; Diceware-class passphrase generator | The classes auditors actually found (Critical and Moderate) | None | Continuous; first before any external audit |
| 4 | **Hybrid manifest signature**: Ed25519 (minisign, kept) plus ML-DSA-65 over the same bytes, both required to verify | Prospective forgery once a CRQC exists; procurement checklists | Manifest schema change under a new `sealdrop-manifest/2` identifier; old verifiers still check the Ed25519 half | 2029 to 2031, gated on an audited Rust ML-DSA crate |
| 5 | **Payload format v2**, only on a concrete trigger: Cobblestone-128 (key-committing AES-128-GCM chunked encryption) under a `sealdrop-encryption.org/v2` first line, one suite per version, dual-read forever | FIPS/AES mandates; committing payload | Total: stock age cannot open v2 packages, so this breaks spec goal 2 by design | Not scheduled |
| Reject | Cipher cascades, "paranoid modes", runtime `cipher=` fields, AES-GCM-as-age-v1 | Nothing that any cited source rates as a live risk | Breaks interop and invites downgrade attacks | Never |

Three details in the table carry most of the risk. First, the PQ mode must be all-or-nothing per file; a package with both `age1pq` and `x25519` stanzas is classically breakable at the X25519 stanza and should be refused by the encoder, mirroring age's own SHOULD NOT. The format cost is tolerable: each PQ stanza adds about 1.5 KB of base64 and PQ recipient strings run to roughly 2,000 characters ([age README](https://github.com/FiloSottile/age)), which matters for the manifest and CLI UX but not for file size, and stays well inside sealdrop's 46 KiB header budget. Second, the signature item cannot be done inside minisign: asked on 29 August 2026 whether it was time for ML-DSA, the maintainer answered "No" and "probably not ... ML-DSA" ([minisign #102](https://api.github.com/repos/jedisct1/minisign/issues/102/comments)), so sealdrop needs its own dual-signature container or a sidecar, in the composite style NIST IR 8547 §3.2.2 accommodates and OpenSSH 10.4 ships experimentally as `mldsa44-ed25519` ([OpenSSH 10.4](https://www.openssh.org/txt/release-10.4)). ML-DSA-65 is the right parameter set: 1,952-byte keys and 3,309-byte signatures ([liboqs](https://openquantumsafe.org/liboqs/algorithms/sig/ml-dsa.html)), verification around 0.18 million cycles against Ed25519's 0.27 million ([pq-crystals](https://pq-crystals.org/dilithium/), [ed25519.cr.yp.to](https://ed25519.cr.yp.to/)), and the choice Sigstore made "over SLH-DSA for its smaller signature size" ([Sigstore](https://blog.sigstore.dev/post-quantum-2025/)). The gating factor is assurance, not standards: FIPS 204 has been final since August 2024, but RustCrypto's `ml-dsa` states it "has never been independently audited" ([docs.rs ml-dsa](https://docs.rs/ml-dsa/latest/ml_dsa/)) and shipped a signature-acceptance bug, CVE-2026-24850, fixed only in 0.1.0-rc.4 ([OSV](https://osv.dev/vulnerability/CVE-2026-24850)); FN-DSA is off the table until FIPS 206 exists, which NIST's September 2026 redesign to fixed-point signing has pushed further out ([pqc-forum](https://groups.google.com/a/list.nist.gov/g/pqc-forum/c/7z-y5qp-W2Y)). Third, if item 5 is ever triggered, Cobblestone is the design to adopt rather than anything bespoke: it is age's author's current chunked scheme, derives key, base nonce and a 32-byte commitment by HKDF-Expand, binds context in the KDF so AAD "MUST be empty", and already has Go and pyca/cryptography implementations ([C2SP chunked-encryption](https://github.com/C2SP/C2SP/blob/main/chunked-encryption.md)). It must sit under a sealdrop version line, as the age author asked of forks ("Pretty please change the version line in the header"), and the manifest should name the suite only for diagnostics, never for runtime selection, following the WireGuard and PASETO pattern that Arciszewski and Soatok argue for against in-band agility ([Paragon IE](https://paragonie.com/blog/2019/10/against-agility-in-cryptography-protocols), [Soatok](https://soatok.blog/2022/08/20/cryptographic-agility-and-superior-alternatives/)).

Two uncertainties should be stated rather than smoothed over. The rage 0.13.0 date is unknown, and the only third-party Rust X-Wing recipient draft is unreviewed by the maintainer ([rage #598](https://github.com/str4d/rage/issues/598)); the plugin bridge is the safe interim, with vendoring a last resort. And the hashcat-derived passphrase arithmetic is scaled from a 16 MiB benchmark vector, so a direct `hashcat -b -m 8900` run at age-equivalent parameters would sharpen the UI's entropy guidance before it is published.

## Conclusion

The owner's instinct that a 10-year package needs more than today's defaults is correct; the instinct that the cipher is the place to add it is not. The research reframes "stronger encryption" from a question about ChaCha20's 20 rounds into a question about which keys an adversary in 2035 can recover, and the answer is the X25519 key-wrap stanza, nothing else. That reframing changes the engineering plan from a risky payload rewrite with total interop loss into a mostly additive programme: PQ-only recipients at the stanza layer that age has already standardised, a commitment field that stock age ignores, custody and parser discipline that the audit record says is where tools fail, and a hybrid signature on the 2031 cadence. The payload layer stays exactly as age specified it, which is what keeps every package openable by any age v1 implementation for the whole decade.

The one genuinely new insight is a timing one. Because the confidentiality clock on harvest-now-decrypt-later started the day the first X25519-only package left a sender's machine, sealdrop's PQ decrypt capability is late relative to its own 10-year promise even though it is early relative to the 2030 mandates. Everything about the roadmap should be read in that light: the Rust ecosystem's missing `age1pq` identity is sealdrop's most important open dependency, and the plugin bridge is worth shipping before rage 0.13 rather than after.
